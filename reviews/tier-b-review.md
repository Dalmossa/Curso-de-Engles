## 0. WHAT THIS REVIEW IS, AND WHAT IT IS NOT

This must be read before the verdicts, because the verdicts are only as good as the process that
produced them, and the process has a known defect.

**It is not an independent expert review.** I authored the deck being reviewed. That is a conflict of
interest, and no amount of rigour in the criteria removes it — I know which sentences I stretched, and
knowing is exactly what makes an author a bad reviewer of their own work.

**What it actually is:** a structured adversarial pass against four pre-declared criteria, applied
item by item, with the reasoning recorded so a second party can disagree with each verdict
individually. That has real value — it found defects that a casual read missed — but it does **not**
discharge the request for expert review, and it should not be recorded as if it did.

**What would discharge it:** one qualified ELT professional with Portuguese as an L2 reviewing `content-map.md` §11.2
independently. The verdicts below are a candidate list for that reviewer, not a substitute.

### 0.1 The four criteria

| # | Criterion | A failure means |
|---|-----------|-----------------|
| **C1** | The PT form is one a Brazilian speaker actually uses, in a way that produces this error | The item tests an error nobody makes |
| **C2** | The declared "correct EN for the PT intent" is actually correct | The item is miskeyed — worse than no item |
| **C3** | The declared "EN actually means" gloss is accurate | The item teaches a false fact |
| **C4** | It really is a false cognate, not a true cognate / polysemy / derivation / unkeyable pair | The item is filed under a label it does not belong to, and inflates the deck's apparent size |

**C4 is where most of the damage is.** Five of the fourteen turned out to be **true** cognates — pairs
where the English and Portuguese words mean the same thing. A deck of false cognates containing true
cognates is not a slightly noisy deck; it is a deck whose headline number (30 items) overstates its
usable content.

## 2. THE EIGHT FAILURES, ITEM BY ITEM

### B4 `confer` — void as a false cognate (C2, C4)
`confer` is not a standalone English word. It is a bound root (*confer-*, as in *confer, conference,
conferment*). It cannot be the target of a false-cognate item, because a false cognate requires a
learner-facing lexical form on both sides, and there is no such form here. The deck's own note
already said this — "flagged so it is authored as a word-formation item" — and then left it in the
cognate table anyway, where it does not belong.
**Disposition:** remove from this deck. It is a legitimate word-formation item (`confer` vs
*conference* vs *conference* in the meeting sense) and belongs in a different deck with different
distractor rules.

### B5 `patio` — wrong gloss and not a false cognate (C3, C4)
EN *patio* and PT *pátio* are **the same thing**: a paved open area beside a building. That is a
**true** cognate. The item only worked if the senses diverged, and they do not.
Separately, the declared gloss "terraço (exterior)" is **factually wrong** — *terraço* is a rooftop or
terrace. EN *patio* is at ground level. The gloss imports a confusion the item does not have.
**Disposition:** drop. If the polysemy of *pátio* (school yard, ship deck, mining yard) is worth
teaching, it is a Portuguese-side vocabulary item, not an L1-interference item.

### B6 `census` — a true cognate, and the item says so (C4)
EN *census* and PT *censo* both mean a population count. Identical meaning, shared root.
The damage is visible in the deck's own row: "EN means: recenseamento." *Recenseamento* **is** a
synonym of *censo*. The item's own gloss proves the two words agree, and then files the pair as a
false cognate. The declared trap (*senso* = *sense*) has no path to the error either.
**Disposition:** drop.

### B8 `mix` — true cognate, polysemy only (C4)
PT *mexer* and EN *mix* share the primary meaning "misturar." The senses *do* diverge at the margin
(PT *mexer* also = "tocar"), which makes this a **polysemy** item. Filed as a false cognate it
implies the primary meanings disagree, which is false.
**Disposition:** drop from the cognate deck. The polysemy ("did you mean *mix* or *touch*?") is a
legitimate A2 vocabulary item and can be reassigned there.

### B9 `serial` — unkeyable: both answers are valid English (C2) 🔴
This is the most serious failure, because it is a **miskey**, not a mislabel.

The item asks a learner who means "série" to choose between `serial` and `series`. **But `serial` is a
real English word** meaning "of or relating to a series," and it is also a correct noun (*TV serial*,
*newspaper serial*). A learner writing `serial` for a context that happens to call for the plural noun
has not made an error the item can detect.

There is no defensible key. Under `assessment-item-spec.md` §5.2, "key share ≈ 1/n_options after
investigation → investigate the key before anything else," and here the key does not exist in the first
place.

**Disposition:** drop. The underlying confusion is real — learners confuse the adjective *serial* with
the noun *series* — but the item must be rebuilt as a usage/collocation item where the context forces
one form (e.g. a blank requiring the plural noun).

### B10 `switch` — the trap form is not producible (C1)
The declared trap is "chave (de dobradiça)" — PT *chave* meaning *hinge*. PT *chave* does not mean
*hinge*; PT *dobradiça* does. A learner would never produce this error, because the premise is false.
Meanwhile EN *switch* ↔ PT *chave* is a **correct** pairing in the electrical sense.
**Disposition:** drop. There is no error to detect.

### B12 `eventual` — redundant with Tier A and misfiled (C4)
EN *eventual* and PT *eventual* substantially overlap ("que pode acontecer" ↔ "possible but not
certain"). The residual confusion is an **ambiguity internal to English** (does *eventual* mean
"finally" or "possible"?), not an L1 transfer. It also duplicates Tier A's A2 item on ***eventually***,
which already carries the Portuguese *eventualmente* trap and is source-derived.
**Disposition:** drop as a standalone item. Harvest the "final vs. possible" ambiguity as a distractor
inside the Tier A *eventually* item, where it adds discrimination without a new source claim.

### B14 `performance` — true cognate in the primary sense (C3, C4)
EN *performance* and PT *desempenho* both mean "how well something works." The deck selects only the
minority theatrical sense ("apresentação, espetáculo") and then invents a requirement not in the
Portuguese: the declared fix is **"current performance"**, but PT *desempenho* imposes no such
requirement.
**Disposition:** drop.

## 3. THE SIX THAT SURVIVE

These passed all four criteria. Details on what each is worth:

### B1 `sensible` / PT *sensível* → *sensitive* — KEEP
The canonical case. PT *sensível* = sensitive; EN *sensible* = reasonable/judicious. A learner writing
`sensible` about a person's feelings is understood, roughly, and understood *wrongly* — the worst kind
of error, because it looks successful. High frequency in professional settings ("a sensible comment").
**Interference value: high. Frequency: high.**

### B3 `assist` / PT *assistir* → *watch / attend* — KEEP
Textbook false cognate, and the direction of the error is asymmetric in a useful way: PT *assistir* =
assistir a uma aula → a learner may write `assist` for "attend the training," which is the opposite of
the intended meaning. Directly relevant to a tech audience, where "assisted support" and "attendance"
both recur.
**Interference value: high. Frequency: high.**

### B7 `solicitor` / PT *solicitante* → *applicant* — KEEP
A **root-level** false cognate: PT *solicitante* and EN *applicant* share the Latin root *solicitare*,
but the English word for the PT concept is not *solicitor* — it is a UK-legal term (a lawyer) and, in
US usage, a canvasser. Useful precisely because B2-confusions about "solicit" are common in contract
and legal-adjacent work.
**Interference value: medium-high. Frequency: low-medium.** Include for coverage, not volume.

### B11 `offender` / PT *ofendido* → *victim* — KEEP
A **derivation-polarity** trap, and the most linguistically interesting survivor. PT *ofendido* is the
**patient** (the one offended); EN *offender* is the **agent** (the one who offends). Same root,
opposite roles. This is the only item in the deck that tests a grammatical-polarity error rather than
a lexical one, so it discriminates a different failure mode.
**Interference value: medium-high (unique). Frequency: medium.**

### B13 `discipline` / PT *disciplina* → *self-control* — KEEP
PT *disciplina* spans two senses — "subject area / rules" and "self-restraint." EN *discipline* spans
"field of study / punishment" and does **not** include self-restraint. A learner translating "ter
disciplina" as "have discipline" produces a sentence that is grammatical and means something different.
Good polysemy-discrimination item.
**Interference value: medium. Frequency: high.**

### B2 `secure` — RECLASSIFY, do not drop
The trap is real (PT *seguro* covers both "safe/secure" and "insurance"), but EN *secure* and PT
*seguro* **are** cognates in the adjective sense. What the item actually tests is a **false friend**:
the PT noun *seguro* (an insurance policy) has no cognate in EN *secure*; it is EN *insurance*.
Also, the declared trap gloss "seguro (objeto)" is poor — it should be **"seguro (apólice, substantivo)"**
to name the actual contrast.
**Disposition:** move to a false-friend deck, with the noun sense made explicit. Retains its value; the
label was wrong.

## 5. WHAT THIS DOES AND DOES NOT UNBLOCK

### It does **not** move the six survivors into the diagnostic CAT.

This is the important part, and it confirms rather than overturns `content-map.md` §11.0 **Decision C**.

Decision C argued that expert review is the wrong gate for admitting an authored item to a
placement test, because the item's difficulty `b` is a claim about how entrenched an interference is
in the learner's L1 population — a distributional quantity that **no amount of linguistic judgement can
identify**. This review is a demonstration of that, not a counterexample to it: the six survivors are
items I can defend as *linguistically well-formed*. That is a statement about the sentences. It says
nothing about their difficulty, and difficulty is what the CAT consumes.

| Gate | Status for the 6 survivors |
|------|--------------------------|
| Linguistic well-formedness (C1–C4) | **PASSED** — this review |
| Source provenance | `authored` — never `source_derived` |
| CAT admission | **still requires n ≥ 200 responses** (`assessment-item-spec.md` §3.2), not review |
| Practice packs (L1/L2/L3) | **cleared** |
| Shadow CAT | **cleared** — it does not report a placement number |

### The reclassified item needs a new home

B2 moves out of the false-cognate deck. `content-map.md` §11.2 should record it under a false-friend
heading, and the 8 void items should be deleted rather than left in place with a warning, so that the
deck's item count equals its usable count.

### Replacements are still needed

Five usable authored items do not close the coverage gap that motivated authoring Tier B in the first
place. `content-map.md` §11.0 Decision C notes that Tier A's 16 items are the high-discrimination
cluster; Tier B's purpose was to widen L1-interference coverage beyond the corpus. That purpose is
**now 57% unmet**, and the honest options are:

1. **Source more false cognates** from a permissively-licensed reference work rather than authoring
   them, which removes the failure mode entirely. This is the direction the external-material work is
   already going.
2. **Author a replacement batch** and run the same four-criteria pass *before* the items enter the
   deck, not after. The pass costs about an hour; authoring 14 items without it cost eight items.

Option 1 is preferred. Option 2 is acceptable only with the pass moved upstream.

## 6. REPRODUCING THIS REVIEW

The criteria are mechanical enough to re-run, and re-running them on a replacement batch is the
cheapest quality control available in this project.

```
C1  Is the PT form attested in BR usage and does it plausibly produce this error?
C2  Is the declared key the only defensible answer? (Both answers valid = unkeyable = drop)
C3  Is the EN gloss accurate, in the sense the item relies on? (Check the sense is the primary one)
C4  Do the primary meanings of the EN and PT forms actually differ? (If they agree = true cognate)
```

C2 and C4 caught 7 of the 8 failures. Both are answerable by a careful reader with a dictionary and no
specialist training — which is itself the finding: **most of this deck's defects were not subtle, and a
pass before authoring would have caught them.**
